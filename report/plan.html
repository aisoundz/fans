Playing

A live-show engagement product for Live Nation, built on an engine that already runs. What it is, why an artist says yes, what the science says it does to a fan's memory, and how it becomes a nine-figure business.

Prototype built & playable fansgametime.com secured Sibling of Stats Gametime

Live Nation put 159 million people in rooms last year. For roughly 101 minutes of every one of those nights, nothing is happening on stage — and every one of those people is holding a phone.

Fans Gametime is a game that lives only in those minutes. It never interrupts a song. It asks you about the show you are standing in — what opened it, how many dancers, what he was wearing — and the artist, not a bookmaker, pays out. And because being asked is how memory is made, it does something no second-screen product has ever claimed: it makes the concert itself harder to forget.

The three rules the product is built on

1
The set is sacred
No question ever fires while a song is playing. This is enforced in the code, not in a policy document, and it is the single reason an artist lets us near their audience.
2
Nothing you can look up
Every question resolves from something visible from where you are standing. No trivia, no discography quiz, no Wikipedia. If a person at home could answer it, it does not ship.
3
The artist pays, not a bookmaker
Entry is free, permanently. Prizes come from the artist and cost them almost nothing: the setlist off the monitor wedge, signed. Soundcheck in the next city. Your name on the LED wall for twelve seconds. A merch code at 60 points, which pays for itself before the fan leaves the building.
Heard It — the one live mechanic

Twice a night, on a moment the whole room is already looking up for — the band walking off, a cover starting — one card appears for twenty seconds and never repeats. Answer and pocket it. If you miss it, it is gone. A card that nags a fan through a song is a card that gets the product banned from a tour, so it cannot exist.

The technical spine

The setlist is the feed

Stats Gametime works because ESPN publishes a live, machine-readable game feed and we wrote 84 resolvers against it. The first question anyone technical will ask about a concert version is the right one: a show has no feed. How does a question resolve?

It has a feed. It is printed on paper and taped to a monitor wedge, and it exists before the show starts.

The case nobody else can make

We do not record
the memory. We make it.

Every second-screen pitch Live Nation has ever heard promises engagement. This one promises something measurable and much larger: that a fan who plays remembers the show better, for longer, and more fondly than a fan who does not — and that a better-remembered show is a ticket bought again.

That is not a marketing claim. It is a stack of some of the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology, and this product happens to be shaped exactly like the intervention those findings describe.

1
Being asked beats watching. This is the whole product.
The testing effect is one of the most robust results in memory research: retrieving information produces substantially better long-term retention than re-studying it, and the advantage grows as the delay lengthens. Tests enhance later retention more than additional study of the same material, even with no feedback. Roediger & Karpicke, 2006
So: scrolling your own concert photos is re-study. Being asked "what opened the show" is retrieval. Only one of those makes the night stick — and the industry currently offers only the first.
2
An answer you produce outlives an answer you are handed
The generation effect: material a person generates themselves is remembered better than the identical material read passively. Slamecka & Graf, 1978 And under levels of processing, attending to meaning encodes far more deeply than attending to surface. Craik & Lockhart, 1972
So: "how many dancers were on stage for Pump It" forces effortful, semantic processing of four seconds a passive viewer would have glanced straight past. That is the mechanism, stated plainly.
3
Asked in the building, encoded with the building
Encoding specificity: recall is strongest when the retrieval context matches the encoding context — demonstrated most famously with divers who learned word lists underwater and recalled them best underwater. Godden & Baddeley, 1975
So: the question must be asked in the room, during the show. The volume, the lights, the people either side of you all get bound into the memory trace. A recap email the next morning gets none of that, which is precisely why every recap email has failed.
4
We get to write the ending — and the ending is what gets remembered
The peak–end rule: people judge an experience almost entirely by its most intense moment and by how it ended, and are strikingly insensitive to how long it lasted (duration neglect). Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier, 1993; Redelmeier & Kahneman, 1996
So: today a great concert ends with twenty-five minutes shuffling toward a car park. That flat, mildly irritating final note is what the brain files as "the end". The walk-out round replaces it with a payoff, a leaderboard, a prize and a share. We are not decorating the memory of the show — we are improving the show's remembered value, which is the number that decides whether they buy the next ticket.
5
Then we ask again, on a schedule, inside an app they already open
Retrieval spread across time beats retrieval crammed together — the spacing effect, and repeated retrieval at expanding intervals is the single best-evidenced way to stop a memory decaying. Karpicke & Roediger, 2007
So: On Repeat asks you again at day 9, day 30 and day 90, on Spotify, between two tracks. That is a textbook expanding-retrieval schedule delivered by a product hundreds of millions of people open daily — and it costs the DSP one card.
6
And the retrieval cue was already built. By the artist.
Music is one of the most potent cues for autobiographical memory known, and music-evoked autobiographical memories are unusually emotional and unusually vivid. Janata et al., 2007 Musical cues produce a more pronounced reminiscence bump than film cues do. Belfi & Jakubowski, 2021
So: we do not need to invent a way to trigger the memory. The artist wrote it, the fan plays it fourteen times a month, and we simply attach the night to it. Nobody else in live entertainment owns both ends of that loop.
7
A concert is already the most arousing thing most people do all month
Emotionally arousing events are consolidated into long-term memory more strongly than neutral ones. A small stake and a twenty-second clock, applied at the exact moment of encoding, push an already-high-arousal event higher. McGaugh, 2000
So: this is the rare intervention whose ideal laboratory conditions are the normal operating conditions of a Live Nation show.
The chain, end to end

Attention → you are asked something only a person in the room can answer → retrieval lays the trace down harder than watching ever could → the ending is engineered instead of accidental → the song becomes the cue → spaced retrieval on the DSP stops it fading → a year later the fan can still describe the night → they buy the next ticket.

A fan who cannot remember a show has no reason to go again. That is the quiet leak in a $25 billion business, and this is the first product shaped to plug it.

The honest caveat — say this in the room before they say it to you

None of these studies were run at a concert. Every mechanism above is robust, replicated and general, but the application is a hypothesis until somebody measures it. So make measuring it part of the pilot, and make it the thing you offer Live Nation that no agency can:

Unaided setlist recall at day 30 and day 90 — players versus a matched control from the same show, same section, same night. If players recall more songs, in better order, we will have the first real evidence that a live event can be made more memorable by design. That is a paper, a press cycle, a defensible moat, and a number that reprices every ticket Live Nation sells. It costs two survey emails.

The other half

On Repeat

The venue is where the memory is made. The DSP is where it is kept alive — and it is also, commercially, the larger surface. Spotify and Live Nation already work together on ticket access for Premium subscribers; this is the engagement product that sits on top of that pipe.

Between two tracks, while a fan is already listening, one card: "You were at the Forum on the 26th. What opened that show?" Three seconds. No interruption to the music that isn't already there in the crossfade.

Why the two halves need each other

  • Carry-in points. What you earn on Spotify in the week before a show you walk into the venue holding. That is why people arrive already playing instead of having to be recruited at the door — it solves the cold-start problem that kills every venue app.
  • Spaced retrieval. The DSP questions are what stop the show fading. Without them the venue game is a fun night; with them it is a memory system.
  • Pre-show homework. Learn the tour's setlist shape on Spotify, arrive able to compete. The artist's catalogue becomes study material, and streams go up.
  • Year-round retention. A fan goes to three shows a year and opens Spotify daily. The venue is the peak; the DSP is the heartbeat.
The flywheel, in one line

Listen (daily, on the DSP) → buy (Ticketmaster) → play the gaps (in the room) → remember (the walk-out, then spaced retrieval) → listen more (the songs now carry a night) → buy again. Fans Gametime is the only product that touches all five, and the same engine runs every one of them.

Growth

Where the virality actually is

Honest ranking. Two of these are structural and would work on night one. Four are plausible and unproven — treat them as hypotheses to test in the pilot, not as a plan.

Structural
The parking-lot post Twenty thousand people, standing still, twenty-five minutes, phone in hand, freshly scored, still buzzing. The share card is tonight's setlist with your score on it. There is no other moment in consumer software where that many people are simultaneously idle, emotional and holding a fresh result. Nobody has ever built for it.
Structural
The wall Top three names go up on the LED between the set and the encore. Everyone in an arena films the LED wall. Your product's user-generated content is shot by twenty thousand cameras, posted for free, and carries the artist's reach rather than yours — while the winners get twelve seconds in front of a stadium, which is a prize that costs literally nothing to produce.
Untested
Section versus section An arena is already divided into teams and no product has ever used it. Floor versus the 200s. The room mechanic already exists in Stats Gametime; here the geography comes free with the ticket.
Untested
The support act finally gets measured Three thousand people watch an opener and nobody counts. Small artists have the loudest and most under-served audiences in music, and they will talk about the first product that proved anyone was watching. This is also the cheapest place to run the first pilot.
Untested
"What was he wearing" Outfit content is already the most-posted category of concert coverage that exists. A question shaped like a screenshot gets screenshotted.
Untested
The nine-day rematch Spotify asks you again while the song is playing. A re-share trigger with no ad spend, arriving at the moment the memory is warmest.
And the part that matters more than all six

Stats Gametime's one real problem, measured honestly, is acquisition. Retention is 2.8 nights a person with no push notifications and no streaks; the audience has never been the issue — finding it has. Live Nation is 159 million people a year walking into rooms we would already be standing in.

That is the deal in one sentence: we bring an engine that works and a game people come back to; they bring the only distribution in live music that matters. Do not go into that room selling an audience. Go in selling a use for theirs.

Why us

The engine is already running

Fans Gametime is not a slide. It is a question bank and a resolver bolted onto a system that has been in production since July, and the honest ledger looks like this — put it in the room exactly this way, because they will find it anyway and finding it themselves is what kills a deal.

Proven
The engine works, at multi-room scale Stats Gametime has run twenty-six game nights across six leagues, with 84 live resolvers, an autonomous host that opens rooms on a schedule, a 100+ check pre-deploy QA gate, voice play, Spanish, and a nightly shadow backtest that graded 143 games and 1,846 resolutions with zero invalid.
Proven
People who play, come back 2.8 nights per person with no push notifications, no streaks, no reminders and no marketing of any kind. The mechanic holds attention. That is the hard half and it is done.
Proven
Shipping speed Six leagues, 84 resolvers, voice, two languages and a full QA gate in about six weeks, on a single Jetson on a desk. This prototype was built in an afternoon on top of it.
Not proven
Demand. Say this out loud. Thirteen humans have ever played Stats Gametime, and an audit found that most non-basketball rooms were played by the founder alone. Quality went from 42% to 100% over eight days while the audience stayed at three. Product quality and audience size are uncorrelated in this product's own data. That is not a reason to be quiet — it is the exact reason this partnership is the right move rather than more building.

Next

Ninety days
to that meeting

The one thing to avoid is walking into Live Nation with a prototype and nothing else. Stats Gametime's own history is the warning: eight days of quality improvements moved the audience from three people to three people. Go into that room with a prototype and one real room, however small. That is the difference between a demo and a company.

1
Weeks 1–2 — make it real to touch
fansgametime.com live on GitHub Pages, same path statsgametime.com took. Harden the prototype against the gamification critique. A one-page leave-behind for the introduction.
2
Weeks 3–4 — the resolver
Setlist ingest (photograph → question bank) and a six-button live panel a tour PA can learn in ninety seconds. This is the only genuinely new engineering.
3
Weeks 5–6 — the first real room
Run it at any show that will have you. A club night, a support slot, a friend's band, forty people. The goal is not revenue — it is the first non-founder players this operation has ever had, and a day-30 recall number.
4
Weeks 7–9 — arena-shaped
Offline queue under load, the share card, the LED-wall feed, and a load test at 5,000 concurrent listeners. The current measured ceiling on the sports build is roughly 78 concurrent and that is the number that must move.
5
Weeks 10–12 — the meeting
Walk in with a working product, one real pilot with real strangers in it, an honest ledger, and a recall measurement nobody else in live music is running.
The ask in the room — keep it this small
  1. Ten dates on one tour. Any size, no fee, no exclusivity.
  2. One production contact who can photograph the setlist at soundcheck.
  3. Three names on the LED wall between the set and the encore.
  4. Permission to measure recall at day 30 and day 90, players versus control.

Four things, none of which costs Live Nation money, and the fourth of which gives them a number no competitor has. That is a yes-shaped ask.
